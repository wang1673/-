Abstract
Music style transfer (MST) aims to reinterpret existing
musical pieces in new stylistic forms while maintaining
their melodic coherence. Conventional approaches condi-
tioned on text or audio overlook the profoundly multimodal
character of musical style.
Visual ambience – reflected
in color, lighting, and composition – encodes affective at-
tributes that parallel timbre, rhythm, and harmony, which,
however, remain underexplored in MST context. We intro-
duce a flow-based, inversion-free framework for multimodal
music style transfer that unifies textual and visual guidance.
Our approach tackles two challenges: (1) capturing cross-
modal semantics beyond language through a dual-encoder
fusion module that merges CLIP- and ViT-derived embed-
dings, and (2) preserving melodic identity using a differ-
entiable normalized chroma constraint that regulates pitch-
class consistency along the generative flow. We reorganize
and extend the MeLBench and MusicCaps collections into a
genre-structured multimodal dataset to support style-aware
analysis. Quantitative and perceptual evaluations demon-
strate that our approach achieves superior control, struc-
tural fidelity, and cross-modal expressiveness, underscoring
the role of visual perception in music generation. Our demo
is available online.

In particular, the visual modality remains a largely un-

Visual imagery, much like music, tells an affective story –

Despite these cross-modal correspondences, current mu-

explicitly. Most rely on textual prompts or reference au-

they fail to capture finer stylistic nuances such as color tone,
spatial composition, or emotional texture – qualities that are
more naturally expressed through visual media. This limi-
tation motivates our first research challenge: how can vi-

fectively represented and utilized for style transfer?
Meanwhile, advances in inversion-free editing [23,

Un-
like diffusion-based inversion, which introduces stochas-
tic noise during reconstruction, inversion-free flow models
establish deterministic transport paths between source and
target distributions. These continuous mappings preserve
global coherence while allowing localized stylistic variation
– an essential property for music, where melodic and rhyth-
mic integrity should be maintained. However, even within
this structure-preserving formulation, abrupt stylistic shifts
may still distort melodic patterns. This exposes our second
challenge: how to preserve pitch and rhythmic identity un-
der large stylistic transformations within an inversion-free
generative framework?
To address these challenges, we propose a unified mul-
timodal MST framework grounded in inversion-free flow
editing [23], as illustrated in Fig. 1. First, we introduce
a cross-modal fusion module that directly integrates vi-
sual embeddings with textual semantics, enabling percep-
tion and translation of non-verbal stylistic cues – such as
ambience, tone, and spatial mood – into coherent musical
expression. Second, to stabilize melodic structure, we in-
corporate a normalized chroma-based constraint that pre-
serves pitch-class consistency during generation, ensuring
structural fidelity while allowing flexible stylistic adapta-
tion. In addition, we construct a new multimodal dataset
of aligned triplets (music, text, and image) to facilitate sys-
tematic studies of cross-modal conditioning in music style
transfer. In summary, our main contributions are threefold:
• A multimodal MST task.
We introduce the multi-
modal music style transfer (MST) task, which promotes
a cross-modal cognitive perspective by aligning musical,
visual, and linguistic modalities within a coherent repre-
sentational paradigm for MST.
• Non-verbal stylistic representation.
We propose a
cross-modal fusion module that integrates visual em-
beddings with textual semantics, effectively capturing
non-verbal ambience cues, such as color, tone, and spa-
tial mood, that are critical to stylistic perception.
• Inversion-free flow with melodic regularization. We
develop an inversion-free flow-based editing approach
equipped with a normalized chroma constraint, achieving
structure-preserving and melody-consistent style trans-
formations.
• A new MST benchmark.
We reorganize and extend
MeLBench and MusicCaps into a more structured mul-
timodal corpus with refined captions and 16-genre anno-
tations, establishing a new benchmark for style-aware and
multimodal MST evaluation.
2. Related Work
Generative-Music Creation. Music, as a uniquely expres-
sive art form, has long attracted research interest in its com-
putational creation methods. Early attempts at music gen-
eration relied on symbolic rules and probabilistic models
such as Markov chains and HMMs [16, 47, 58] could cap-
ture simple melodic patterns but lacked expressive control
and scalability. With the advent of deep learning, gener-
ative models based on VAEs and GANs greatly advanced
the field. MusicVAE [48] introduced a hierarchical struc-
ture to model long-term dependencies; MuseGAN [8] and
MidiNet [62] demonstrated GAN-based multi-track gener-
ation.
More recently, diffusion models [18, 29, 32, 35]
have achieved state-of-the-art fidelity by iteratively refining
noise under semantic or textual guidance, offering strong
controllability and realism.
Beyond text-only condition-
ing, multimodal approaches such as MelFusion [3], Mus-
Flow [53], and Diff-BGM [26] incorporate visual or cross-
modal cues to enhance stylistic expressiveness in music
generation. However, these models primarily focus on un-
conditional or from-scratch synthesis and lack mechanisms
to preserve melodic and rhythmic content – key to effective
music style transfer.
Music Style Transfer.
Music style transfer (MST) has
gradually evolved into an important branch of genera-
tive music research, aiming to re-render existing pieces

in new stylistic forms while retaining their musical iden-
tity. While earlier studies explored various generative deep
learning paradigms for this task [20, 39], recent progress in
diffusion-based generation [44, 61] has opened new possi-
bilities for high-fidelity and controllable music stylization.
Recent progress in text-conditioned music generation has
further advanced controllable stylization. MuLan [17] in-
troduces a joint audio–text embedding that supports text-
guided re-rendering of a given excerpt. Noise2Music [18]
extends this idea by using a cascade of text-conditioned dif-
fusion models that can be applied to modify an input clip
at high fidelity.
Meanwhile, large-scale language–audio
models such as MusicGen [5] and MusicLM [1] further ex-
tend style music generation by conditioning the process on
both textual prompts and optional melodic inputs. In paral-
lel, diffusion-based instruction-driven frameworks [14, 57]
have been explored for general audio or music editing,
where operations such as Add, Remove, or Replace are
learned from supervised (prompt, input, output) triplets.
Inversion-based Editing Methods.
Beyond generative
music models trained from scratch using text or instruc-
tions, recent studies have explored leveraging pretrained
diffusion priors for unsupervised style transfer and zero-
shot editing.
This direction was first established in the
image domain, where methods such as SDEdit [36] and
Pix2Pix-Zero [43] demonstrated that perturbations in noise
space can enable semantic edits while preserving structural
fidelity. These methods rely on diffusion inversion [19, 37]
to reconstruct latent noise trajectories from real samples and
enable semantic manipulation.
Building on these developments, diffusion inversion has
been adapted to the audio domain for tasks such as tim-
bre conversion and music resynthesis [27, 30, 34], show-
ing that pretrained diffusion priors can support zero-shot
or text-driven music editing.
However, inversion re-
quires stochastic multi-step reconstruction, making it com-
putationally expensive and susceptible to accumulated er-
rors [25] that cause temporal drift in long-form audio. To
alleviate these limitations, inversion-free flow-based frame-
works [23, 60] learn source-to-target transformations di-
rectly in latent space, offering a more stable and efficient
alternative.
While such formulations have shown strong
performance in visual domains, their potential for struc-
tured musical transformation remains underexplored.
In
this work, we extend the inversion-free flow paradigm to
music style transfer, introducing melody-preserving con-
straints and multimodal conditioning for rhythm-consistent
and visually guided musical editing.
3. Preliminary
Music Style Transfer. Music style transfer (MST) aims
to generate a stylized audio xsty from a source clip xsrc,
preserving melody and rhythm while altering stylistic char-
acteristics [13]. Traditional MST relies on a single refer-
ence or domain target, whereas our extended formulation
introduces multimodal style conditions (T, I), where text T
provides explicit intent and image I conveys complemen-
tary visual ambience. This multimodal conditioning enables
finer control and more expressive style reinterpretation.

4. Methodology
In this section, we introduce a multimodal fusion module
that integrates visual and textual cues into the audio back-
bone (Section 4.1), followed by an inversion-free editing
framework with chroma guidance (Section 4.2) to preserve
melodic and rhythmic structure. Together, they form a uni-
fied pipeline for multimodal music style transfer (Fig. 2).
4.1. Cross-Adapter for Visual-Textual Conditioning
Style information in music is often conveyed not only by
sound but also by visual ambience – color palettes, light-
ing, composition, and texture – that language alone cannot
fully describe. This raises a natural question: how can im-
age information be effectively integrated into the model?

A straightforward solution is to use existing text-to-audio
models by converting image descriptions into text. How-
ever, from an information-theoretic standpoint [52], this in-
troduces information loss. Let I be the reference image,
C = g(I) its caption, and Z the generated audio. Since
I →C →Z is a Markov chain, the data-processing in-
equality gives I(I; Z) ≤I(I; C), meaning that the cap-
tion C can only lose, not gain, stylistic information from I.
Hence, conditioning on visual features provides richer style
cues than using image captions alone. Empirically, our ex-
periments indicate that captioning an image often fails to
capture fine-grained aesthetic cues relevant to musical style.
Motivated by this, we incorporate visual features alongside
textual prompts, allowing the model to perceive and trans-
late musical style in a more holistic and expressive manner.
To support such multimodal conditioning, a flexible and
efficient generative backbone is essential. We adopt Make-
An-Audio 3 (MAA3) [65], an enhanced transformer-based
diffusion architecture that extends DiT with improved nor-
malization, frequency-aware attention, and adaptive modu-
lation for stronger conditional control. Unlike convolutional
backbones, MAA3 tokenizes latent features, enabling effi-
cient modeling of long-range temporal dependencies—an
essential property for music. In the music generation setup,
audio waveforms are first converted into Mel-spectrograms
via STFT and encoded by a lightweight audio VAE into la-
tent tokens. A pretrained Flan-T5 text encoder [4] provides
semantic conditioning through adaptive layer normalization
(AdaLN) in each transformer block, while rotary positional
embeddings (RoPE) capture temporal structure and main-
tain cross-layer alignment. This token-based backbone of-
fers a flexible foundation for incorporating additional con-
ditional streams, making it particularly suitable for our pro-
posed cross-adapter integration of visual information.
Building upon this foundation, we introduce a cross-
adapter mechanizm to integrate visual features into the
transformer without disrupting its pretrained structure.
Specifically, we employ two complementary visual en-
coders – CLIP [45] and ViT [9] – to capture distinct aspects
of visual style. CLIP excels at learning high-level seman-
tic correspondences between vision and language, provid-
ing conceptually aligned embeddings, whereas ViT focuses
on global information [46]. The resulting embeddings are
pooled, linearly projected, and injected into the transformer
via a cross-attention mechanism, enabling the generator to
translate rich non-verbal cues – such as color tone, light-
ing, and mood – into coherent musical expression. For each
transformer block, the main stream and control stream share
the query projection but maintain distinct key and value pro-

4.2. Melody Preservation via Melodic Guidance
While multimodal conditioning enriches stylistic expres-
siveness, it may also disrupt melodic stability. In practice,
we observe that when the model relies solely on seman-
tic cues from text or images, the generated audio can ex-
hibit pitch drift or rhythmic distortion – altering the mu-
sical identity of the source. To mitigate this issue, we in-
troduce a melody guidance mechanism that explicitly con-
strains the pitch-class distribution of the generated signal
into the inversion-free flow framework. We illustrate the
algorithmic details in Algorithm 1.
Our approach leverages the normalized chroma repre-
sentation [2], a compact descriptor of melody that cap-
tures the relative energy distribution across the twelve pitch
classes within an octave. Compared to raw fundamental
frequency (F0) curves, normalized chroma is amplitude-
invariant, timbre-robust, and polyphony-tolerant, providing
a more stable basis for constraining melodic consistency.

5. Experiments and Results
5.1. Experiments Setup
Datasets. We construct a unified multimodal dataset by
merging extended versions of MeLBench [3] and Music-
Caps [1], both normalized into triplets ⟨I, T, M⟩represent-
ing image, text, and music. Each triplet contains a visu-
ally descriptive image I, a caption T describing the musi-
cal intent, and an audio clip M aligned in style and emo-
tion. To enhance stylistic consistency, we apply the stem-
separation model Demucs [6] to remove vocals and re-
tain purely instrumental tracks across both datasets. The
MusicCaps subset is further extended with visually rele-
vant images and enriched using an LLM-assisted annota-
tion pipeline, which classifies each clip into one of 16 mu-
sical genres (e.g., ambient, jazz-fusion, electronic, retro-
pop) and standardizes captions for stylistic coherence, ad-
ditional details are provided in the Appendix. The final cor-
pus provides genre-aware, visually grounded triplets with
richer metadata, enabling fine-grained multimodal condi-
tioning and style-transfer evaluation.
Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate our framework using
both objective and subjective metrics, with detailed pro-
cedures and rating criteria provided in the Appendix. We
evaluate the transferred music in terms of content preserva-
tion and style similarity. The main metrics include Fr´echet
Audio Distance (FAD) [21], Fr´echet Distance (FD) [22],
Image–Music Similarity Metric (IMSM) [3], Fundamen-
tal Frequency Correlation (F0-PCC) [41], and Chroma-CQT
Similarity (CCS) [51]. FAD and FD measure distributional
alignment between generated and target-style audio in per-
ceptual embedding spaces, while IMSM assesses cross-
modal consistency with visual–textual prompts.
F0-PCC
and CCS capture melodic and harmonic preservation, re-
flecting how well pitch and tonal progression are main-
tained. Lower FAD/FD and higher IMSM, F0-PCC, and
CCS indicate better style transfer quality.
We conduct a user study to evaluate the perceptual qual-
ity of the transferred music. Following prior works in text-
to-audio generation [3, 11, 29], three subjective measures
are adopted: overall audio quality (OVL), prompt relevance
(REL), and melodic consistency (MOScon). Fifteen partic-
ipants rated each sample on a 0–100 scale, where higher
scores indicate better perceptual performance in terms of
realism, stylistic relevance, and melody preservation.
Implementation Details. For fair comparison, we adopt
MAA3 [65] as our backbone. The model is trained on L40S
GPUs using the 1.5B backbone with an additional 46.2M
adapter parameters. All baselines are evaluated under iden-
tical settings. The cross-adapter is trained with a learning
rate of 1 × 10−5 and a batch size of 16.
The melody-
guided rate η is set to 1 × 10−4 with a step size of 2 and
a weight λchr of 1. The inference step count is set to 25
following [65].

5.3. Overall Performance
We provide both objective and perceptual comparisons
against representative baselines in Table 1. Compared with
text-to-music generation systems such as MusicLM [1],
MusicGen [5], and TANGO [32], our method performs a
direct transformation rather than synthesis from scratch.
These generative models produce coherent audio but lack
access to source material, making structure-based met-
rics (e.g., F0-PCC, CCS) inapplicable; In contrast, our
inversion-free flow editing preserves temporal and har-
monic structures while enabling fine-grained style adap-
tation under multimodal control.
Compared with Mu-
sicTI [27] and ZETA [34], our method achieves higher
F0-PCC and CCS scores, possibly due to the chroma-
based constraint, which helps maintain pitch stability and
tonal coherence. Among multimodal approaches, only our
method and MelFusion [3] incorporate visual guidance.
While MelFusion benefits from image features, our cross-
adapter fusion achieves stronger alignment between visual
ambience and musical expression, as reflected in the higher
IMSM score.
Additionally, the left panel of Fig. 3 compares Mel-
spectrograms across multimodal prompts. A ideal music
style transfer method should keep the source’s structural
patterns (melodic contours, rhythmic events) while modi-
fying only style-related energy distributions. Our method
achieves this behavior, whereas MeLFusion exhibits notice-
able structural deformation.
5.4. Ablation Study
Effect of the Image Modality. We assess the contribution
of visual information through modality ablation and a coun-
terfactual test. As shown in Table 2, text-only condition-
ing yields coherent transfers but lacks fine-grained stylis-
tic cues.
BLIP-generated captions slightly improve per-
formance but remain inferior to direct visual embeddings,
while image-only conditioning performs the worst, indicat-
ing that visual cues require textual grounding. Combining
text and image achieves the best performance – especially in
IMSM – showing that visual features provide complemen-
tary style information beyond text alone. We further ob-
serve that adding BLIP captions to text+image can degrade
performance, likely due to low-quality or verbose captions
introducing noise into the conditioning space. Furthermore,
the counterfactual test (Fig. 3, right) shows that altering
only the image while keeping the text fixed leads to clearly
different outputs, demonstrating that visual cues indepen-
dently affect the generated style.

6. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced a multimodal, inversion-free
flow framework for music style transfer that unifies vi-
sual and textual semantics within a coherent editing pro-
cess. By combining a dual-encoder fusion module for cross-
modal stylistic cues with a differentiable chroma-based con-
straint for melodic preservation, the model achieves style
transformation while maintaining pitch-class structure and
musical identity. Comprehensive objective and perceptual
evaluations demonstrate consistent improvements in struc-
tural fidelity, stylistic controllability, and multimodal align-
ment.
We further reorganized and extended MeLBench
and MusicCaps into a genre-aware multimodal corpus, en-
abling more rigorous style-aware evaluation. Future direc-
tions include developing lighter flow architectures to pursue
broader multimodal pretraining for more adaptive and inter-
active creative applications.
